# Life Change Habits

Turn the change the user wants into one **habit experiment**. A habit experiment is small enough to run, specific enough to score, and temporary enough to revise without treating a lapse as a verdict on the user.

Work through exactly seven steps in order. Ask one question at a time, offer a recommended answer whenever the available context supports one, and wait for the user's response. If the invocation already answers a question, propose the inferred answer for confirmation instead of asking them to repeat it. Do not advance while the current step is vague.

## 1. Choose one change

Ask what they want to be different and why it matters now. Translate the answer into one observable behaviour under their control. If they bring a bundle of habits, recommend the one with the strongest leverage and park the rest for later.

**Complete when:** the user has confirmed one behaviour and the outcome it is meant to support.

## 2. Find the baseline

Ask what happens today: frequency, timing, place, and the situation that usually prompts the current behaviour. Use an estimate if no tracked data exists; do not turn missing data into a prerequisite.

**Complete when:** the current frequency and one likely cue are explicit.

## 3. Shrink the rep

Define both:

- the **target rep** — the concrete behaviour for an ordinary day;
- the **minimum rep** — a version that still counts on a difficult day.

Both must be observable and finishable. For a goal about stopping or reducing something, make the rep a replacement behaviour rather than mere absence.

**Complete when:** another person could tell unambiguously whether either rep happened.

## 4. Anchor and shape the environment

Build an implementation intention: “After [stable cue], I will [rep] in [place].” Then choose one environment change that makes the desired rep easier or the competing behaviour harder. Prefer changing what is visible, ready, or within reach over relying on motivation.

**Complete when:** the cue, place, and one environment change are named.

## 5. Plan for the likely obstacle

Ask for the obstacle most likely to break the plan. Write one if-then response for it. Add a recovery rule: a missed opportunity is recorded without punishment, and the next normal cue is the restart — no compensating marathon and no waiting for a new week.

**Complete when:** the plan covers one likely obstacle and the first move after a lapse.

## 6. Score a short experiment

Choose one binary measure of the behaviour itself, such as “rep completed: yes/no.” Outcome measures such as weight, mood, or money may be noted, but they do not replace the behaviour measure. Pick a low-friction place to record it, a start date, and an end date that includes at least five real opportunities to perform the habit.

**Complete when:** the measure, tracker, dates, and number of opportunities are explicit.

## 7. Schedule the review

Set a review date. At review, calculate completed opportunities rather than protecting a streak, identify the main source of friction, and choose exactly one response:

- **keep** the plan unchanged;
- **shrink** the target or minimum rep;
- **reshape** the cue or environment;
- **replace** the habit if it does not serve the intended outcome.

If the experiment has not happened yet, do not pretend to perform this review now. Give the user the review questions and a date to return with the data.

**Complete when:** the review date, review questions, and four possible decisions are in the plan.

## Deliver the habit experiment

End with one compact plan in this format:

```markdown
## Habit experiment

- Change I want:
- Why it matters now:
- Baseline:
- Target rep:
- Minimum rep:
- After [cue], I will [rep] in [place].
- Environment change:
- If [likely obstacle], then [response].
- After a lapse:
- Score:
- Track it in:
- Experiment dates:
- Review date:
- At review, ask:
  1. How many real opportunities were there, and how many reps happened?
  2. What created the most friction?
  3. Will I keep, shrink, reshape, or replace the plan?
```

Ask the user to confirm the plan and name the first cue they will act on. Do not add extra habits, motivational speeches, punishment, or invented claims about how long habit formation takes.

## Safety boundary

This skill supports behaviour design, not diagnosis or treatment. If the requested change involves self-harm, an eating disorder, unsafe exercise, medication changes, or withdrawal from alcohol or drugs, do not design a self-managed cessation plan. Encourage qualified support and limit the habit experiment to a safe supporting action, such as arranging an appointment or recording information a professional requested.
